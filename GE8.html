GE8 U1C3

Getting Directions

Unit 1 · In the City

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a directions exchange: each student independently asks “How can I get to [place]?” and answers with a correct short route (“Turn left at the corner.”, “Go straight.”); assess spoken language as well as whether the listener reaches the destination.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] How Did You Get Here? — ask “How do you get to Bilingo?”; students answer with by + transport or on foot, reviewing GE8 U1C2 before moving from transport to directions.
  • [Interactive Game] Title-page Detective — play the title-page animation and ask “What are the friends looking at?”; students identify the map; accept further observations from the animation.
  • [Topical Discussion] Predict the Problem — after watching the title-page animation, ask “Why might the friends need a map?”; accept different predictions and revisit them after the story.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. Where do the friends want to go? [Point to the destination on the map.] → The Bund.
  • 2. What does Lele draw in her notebook? [Point to the notebook.] → A map.
  • 3. At the first corner, should they turn left or right? [Point to the correction.] → Left.
  • 4. How many blocks do they walk straight after the first left turn? [Point to the relevant line.] → Two blocks.
  • 5. What will they see at the Bund? [Point to the final explanation or river on the map.] → The river.
  • 6. What is the story mainly about? → The friends learn the way to the Bund.
B
  • 1. Do you prefer to draw a map or remember the directions? Why? → Accept a choice with a relevant reason, e.g. “I’d draw a map because I might forget.”
  • 2. What mistake does Lele make when she repeats the directions? → She says turn right, but they should turn left at the first corner. Students find the correction in the text.
  • 3. How will they know they have reached the Bund? → They will see the river. Students find the sentence that confirms this.
  • 4. Why does Lele say “Thank you” to the woman? → The woman has helped them understand the way to the Bund. Students support their answer with the final exchange.
C
  • 1. Why does Lele draw a map? → To help the friends remember and follow the directions. Ask students to connect her drawing with the directions in the text.
  • 2. How do you think Lele feels when her friend corrects her? What makes you think so? → Possible answer: embarrassed or sorry, because she says “Oops! Sorry.” Accept other interpretations supported by the picture, animation or dialogue.
  • 3. Your friend gives you directions, but you are not sure whether to turn left or right. What could you say? → Possible answers: “Left or right?” / “Can you say that again?” Accept other clear, polite ways to check.
  • 4. How does the grocery store help them follow the route? → It shows them where to turn right and cross the road. Students identify the matching instruction.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
mapwayalongcornergladstraightstreetturncrossblock (n.)directions

📐 Grammar Focus

1. How + can + subject + get to + place (get stays in base form); synonyms: “Where is the…?” / “Can you tell me the way to…?”. 2. Begin direction instructions with a base verb: walk, turn, cross or go. 3. along + street for movement following a street; cross + road/street for movement across; at the corner for the turning point; for + number + block(s) for distance. 4. then puts familiar route instructions in order.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • How can I get to [place]?
  • Turn left/right at the corner.
  • Go straight.
  • Cross the road/street.
  • Turn left/right at the [place].
  • Walk/Go straight for [number] block(s).

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Guide the Co-teacher — the co-teacher wears an eye mask and asks “How can I get to…?” (TV / my table / [student name]’s table); students (or one student) give directions; the co-teacher occasionally takes a wrong turn for students to correct.
  2. Tourist and Guide — pairs use destination cards with different places marked (draw them or use flashcards); the “tourist” asks “How can I get to…?” and the guide gives directions; swap roles and destinations; add a review question about transport before the directions if appropriate.
  3. City-word Challenge — students draw a target-word card and use it in a meaningful sentence about a map or the story; include all eleven words, not just direction commands — possible sentences include “Can you give me directions?”, “We know the way.” and “I’m glad to help.”; repeat with new sentences rather than copied models.
  4. Video about giving directions in English — watch before the class and decide which one to use for your lesson based on your students’ level. (see below)

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: draw an imaginary neighbourhood with a starting point and at least three destinations; record directions to one destination without naming it — a family member follows the route and guesses the place; then record a guide making one wrong turn and play both roles: stop the guide politely and give the corrected direction.

Healthy Eating

Unit 2 · Food and Drink

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a health-check exchange: each student independently asks “Is [lettuce] good for you?” and answers “Yes, [lettuce] is good for you.” / “No, [fast food] is bad for you.”.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Problem Situation] Lunchbox Choice: the teacher mimes packing lunch — grabs a huge pile of candy, mimes eating it, clutches their stomach; “For lunch? Hmm… is it good for me?”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“Yes! Candy every day!”); students correct in any language; land on “Is candy good for you? — No, it isn’t. It’s bad for you.” — then the teacher mimes happily eating lettuce: “And this?”
  • [Topical Discussion] What Did You Eat Today?: ask students what they ate today or yesterday; accept one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the food words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

—

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
choicehealthyunhealthylettuceoilonionpickpitysaltsauce

📐 Grammar Focus

1. be good for / be bad for + noun (contrast with be good at + noun/verb-ing). 2. be going to (review); choose (v.) / choice (n.) — make a choice.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • Is lettuce good for you? — Yes, lettuce is good for you.
  • Is fast food good for you? — No, fast food is bad for you.

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. The Healthy Run — designate one side of the room “Good for you” and the other “Bad for you”; the teacher holds up a food flashcard and asks “Is [salt] good for you?”; students run to the correct side and say the full sentence (“No, salt is bad for you! It is unhealthy!”); repeat with lettuce, onion, oil and sauce; vary the prompts and check individual accuracy before going faster.
  2. The Chef’s Choice — a restaurant role-play: put food flashcards on a table (the “kitchen”); the teacher is the chef, a student is the customer: “Welcome! It is time to pick your food. What is your choice?” — “I pick the lettuce and the onion.” — “Is that healthy?” — “Yes, it is good for me.” — “Do you want oil and sauce?” — “No, sauce is bad for you!” (or “Yes, just a little!”); swap roles and let the student be the chef.
  3. Blind Menu Guessing — hold a food flashcard above one student’s head so the class sees it but the student cannot; the student asks “Is it healthy?” — the class answers; the student keeps asking “Is it lettuce?” — “Is it an onion?” until they guess; for unhealthy cards the class answers “No, it is bad for you! It is unhealthy!”
  4. Healthy Plate Draw — students draw their own healthy lunch plate with at least four target foods, then present it: “I pick… Is it good for you?” — the class checks each item.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: ask a family member about one food habit — “Is [noodles] good for you?” — and report the answer to the class.

Favourite Foods

Unit 2 · Food and Drink

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a taste-opinion exchange: each student independently says “I think [grapes] taste great/terrible.” about a pictured or named food.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] Great or Terrible?: quick review of last lesson’s check — the teacher mimes eating grapes vs. a fast-food burger and the class asks “Is it good for you?” and answers; then the teacher eats imaginary grapes with a huge smile: “Mmm! Grapes… great? Terrible?”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“I think grapes taste terrible!”); students react and correct in any language; land on “I think grapes taste great.”
  • [Topical Discussion] Foods You Love and Hate: ask students about foods they love or hate; accept faces, gestures and one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the opinion words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

—

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
beefdeliciousgrapeslambporkstrawberrytastyterriblethink
B
kebab

📐 Grammar Focus

1. I think + clause (that is optional); negative: I don’t think + clause. 2. “What food do you think is healthy?” — review of third-person forms.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • I think grapes taste great.
  • I think hamburgers taste terrible.
B
  • — What do you think of [food]?*

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. The Gross Kebab — draw a large skewer on the whiteboard; a student chooses three ingredients from the vocabulary (including joke ones — a kebab worksheet with ingredient lists is in the GE8 U2C6 materials folder) and the class critiques the creation: “I think a pork and strawberry kebab tastes terrible!”; add the review check “Is it healthy?” — “No, it is bad for you.”; challenge them to build a kebab the class thinks tastes delicious. (see below)
  2. Stand Up If You Agree — all students sit; the teacher makes a food opinion (“I think lamb with sugar tastes delicious.”); students who agree stand up, the rest stay seated with crossed arms; the teacher asks one student from each side “What do you think?” and the student answers with the target sentence; let students take turns being the teacher with their own weird food combinations.
  3. Taste Survey — in pairs, students interview each other: “I think [kebabs] taste great. What do you think?”; each student reports one partner’s opinion to the class with the full sentence.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: interview a family member — “I think … tastes great. What do you think?” — and report their opinion to the class.

Different Tastes

Unit 2 · Food and Drink

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a taste-check exchange: each student independently says “[This lemonade] is too [sour].” / “[These waffles] are not [sweet] enough.” about a pictured food.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] Too Much Sugar!: the teacher mimes tasting last lesson’s kebab — “I think it tastes…”; then mimes spooning ten spoons of sugar into lemonade and drinking it — a disgusted face: “It is…? Hmm, what’s the word?”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“Very sweet! Perfect!”); students correct in any language; land on “This lemonade is too sweet.” — then a tiny sip face for “not sweet enough”.
  • [Topical Discussion] Sweet, Sour, Salty: ask students which foods they know that are sweet, sour or salty; accept gestures (yummy/yucky faces) and one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the taste words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. How are Pili's fries? -> They are too salty.
  • 2. What's wrong with Lele's waffles? -> They aren't sweet enough.
B
  • 1.Why doesn't Laki like the taste of coffee? -> It's too bitter.
  • 2. What food does Ola like? -> He likes sweet and salty food.
C
  • 1. Is Ola a good friend? Is he polite?-> No, he eats all his friends' foods. / Accept students' own answers
  • 2. Is it healthy to eat so much sweet food? Why or why not? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
saltybittersoursweetsmelltaste
B
lemonadesugar
C
syrupwaffle

📐 Grammar Focus

1. too + adjective/adverb (excessively); too many/too much + noun. Also: too at the end of an affirmative sentence means "also" (e.g. "I like it, too."). 2. not + adjective/adverb + enough; enough + noun.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • This coffee is too sweet.
B
  • These waffles are not sweet enough.

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Blind Taste Test — put the review food cards (beef, lamb, pork, grapes, strawberry, kebab) face down in pile A and the flavour cards (sugar, syrup, salt, lemon, coffee) face down in pile B (food-and-flavour word cards are in the GE8 U2C7 materials folder); a student draws one card from each pile (e.g. lamb + syrup): “Congratulations, you are eating lamb with syrup. How does it taste?” — “I think it tastes terrible! It is too sweet.”
  2. Too Much! Mimes — the teacher mimes actions with clearly wrong amounts (a whole salt shaker on food, one tiny spoon of sugar in tea, the whole bottle of syrup on waffles). The students produce target structures based on the miming.
  3. Flavor Corners — name the room corners SWEET, SOUR, SALTY and BITTER; call out foods (“Lemonade!” “Coffee!” “Grapes!”); students move to a corner and say the sentence (“Lemonade is too sour for me.” / “Coffee is too bitter.”); ask one student per corner why.
  4. Too Sour (HTML Interactive) -- students choose the correct description of the food on TV, then make a sentence. They can also tell if they like or don't like it. (see below)

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: try a food or drink you've never had before. Express your opinion about it ("These are waffles. I think they taste terrible… They are too sweet! I don't like sweet food, because it is bad for you.").

GE8 U2C8

Dessert Time

Unit 2 · Food and Drink

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a preference exchange: each student independently says “I prefer [smoothies] to [milkshakes].” about a pictured pair.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] Can’t Decide!: the teacher holds two imaginary drinks (or show pictures) that kids know of, e.g., juice and tea and looks torn: “Hmmm... I like juice, but I also like tea. But I like juice more. How to say it?”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“I like juice! I don't like tea!”); students hint in any language; land on “I prefer smoothies to milkshakes.”
  • [Interactive Game] This or That — the teacher names pairs of foods (ice cream vs. cake, noodles vs. rice, smoothie vs. milkshake); students move to a side of the room; the teacher and co-teacher make the dialogue to activate the language (“They prefer ice cream to cake. And they prefer cake to ice cream. Interesting!").

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. What does Laki order? -> Laki orders vanilla pudding.
  • 2. What does Lele prefer -- smoothies or milkshakes? -> Lele prefers smoothies to milkshakes.
B
  • 1. What do you prefer -- smoothies or milkshakes? -> Accept students' own answers. 2. Who buys food for everyone? -> Ola buys dessert for everyone.
  • 2. [Connection to the previous text] Why is Ola full? Why are other kids hungry? -> Because Ola eats the other kids' foods.
C
  • 2. Why does Ola order ice cream sundae and ice cream cone? -> Becase he wants to share dessert with Pili.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
conefullmilkshakeorderprefersharesmoothietreat
B
sundaevanilla

📐 Grammar Focus

1. be going to (review): concept, time markers, structure. 2. prefer A to B = like A more than B; prefer takes a plural or uncountable noun. 3. Phrases: take one’s order; order something for somebody.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • I prefer smoothies to milkshakes.
B
  • — Which do you prefer, [A] or [B]?*

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. This or That (HTML Interactive) — the teacher holds up two flashcards or project the photo deck (GE8 U2C8 materials folder): “Milkshake or smoothie? Go!”; students walk to the left or right side of the room; each side says the full sentence (“I prefer milkshakes to smoothies.”); repeat rapidly with new pairs: cone vs. sundae, vanilla vs. chocolate, waffle vs. cake. (see below)
  2. Preference Chain — sit in a circle; the teachers asks "What do you prefer -- [A] or [B]?"; the first student says “I prefer [A] to [B].” The teacher and the students can use target or familiar foods (from the flashcards or pictures on TV or memory). The fisrt students asks the random student in a circle: “What do you prefer -- [A] or [B]?”; the next student answers and continues the chain.
  3. What Do You Want to Make? (HTML Interactive) -- students choose to make a pizza, sundae, or hot pot, then take turns choosing between two ingredients and saying "I prefer ... to ...". Each choice adds a huge pile of that ingredient with funny sounds and animations, until the class reveals its disgusting final creation. (see below)

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: ask two family members “Do you prefer [X] or [Y]?” and report both answers with full prefer sentences.
