GE7 U1C1

My Week

Unit 1 · School Life

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a routine exchange: each student independently says “I [exercise] every day.”, “I never [do yoga].” and “I usually [practice kung fu].” about themselves, using the target activity words.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Problem Situation] How Many Times?: the teacher mimes doing exercise again and again, day after day, then asks the co-teacher “How often do I exercise?”; the co-teacher answers wrong on purpose (“One time a year!”); students shout the correct idea in any language (“Every day!”) — accept everything, act it out, and land on “I exercise every day.”; repeat the mime crossed with a big X for “I never do yoga.”
  • [Topical Discussion] After School: ask students what they do after school or at the weekend (sports, drawing, games); accept gestures and one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the activity words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

—

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
babysitcopyexerciselook afterwork hardkung fuyogaboringexciting
B
character

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Adverbs of frequency: always (100%), usually (80%), often (70%), sometimes (50%), seldom/rarely (10%), never (0%); also “hardly ever”. 2. Simple present, first person (review): I/we + adverb + verb phrase; I/we + verb + every day. 3. Third-person singular spelling: add -s; add -es after s, x, sh, ch, o; consonant + y → i + es. 4. Negative forms: be + not; don’t/doesn’t + base verb.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • I exercise every day.
  • I never do yoga.
  • I usually practice kung fu.
B
  • — What do you do every day?*

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Boring or Exciting? Debate Corners — call out an activity (“Babysit!”); students move to the BORING side or the EXCITING side of the room; pick one student per side to justify with a full sentence (“I babysit every day. It’s boring!” vs. “I never babysit. It’s exciting!”); push for disagreement and follow-ups.
  2. Frequency Line-Up — put a line on the floor (tape or imagined): one end = EVERY DAY, middle = USUALLY, other end = NEVER; call out activities (“Exercise!” “Copy homework!” “Practice kung fu!”); students place themselves on the line; then students at different points say their sentence (“I usually exercise.”); quick interviews: “Really? You never copy? Never never?”
  3. Copy Cat Chain — sit in a circle; student 1 says “I usually practice kung fu.”; student 2 repeats it and adds their own (“S/he usually practices kung fu. I never babysit.”); the chain grows round the circle — if someone forgets, the chain restarts from the next student (nobody sits out).
  4. Weekly Mimes — the teacher mimes one target activity; the class guesses the word and calls how often (“Every day!” / “Never!”); the teacher confirms in a target sentence (“I look after my little brother every day.”).

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: make three true sentences about your week with every day, usually and never, and read them to a family member.

Habits

Unit 1 · School Life

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a habit exchange: each student independently answers “What does s/he often do?” with “[He] often [watches films].” about a classmate, family member or character.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] My Week, His Week: the teacher mimes last lesson’s routine (exercise, kung fu) and the class says the sentence (“You usually practice kung fu!”); then the teacher points at a student who likes films and stalls: “He… usually? Hmm, I can’t say it!”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“I usually watches films!”); students laugh, correct in any language, and the class lands on “He often watches films.”
  • [Topical Discussion] Family Habits: ask students what their mum, dad or friend often does (watch films, study, exercise); accept one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the habit words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. What does Nani's dad do every Sunday? -> He jogs though the park every Sunday.
  • 1. What do Pili's parents do on Saturdays? -> They often have dinner at a restaurant.
B
  • 1. Why does Pili babysit his sister? -> Because his parents are not at home on Saturdays.
  • 2. Why is Ola's handwriting messy? -> Because he writes quickly.
C
  • 1. Nani's dad jogs through the park every Sunday. It's a good habit. What good habits do your parents or you have? -> Accept students' own answers.
  • 2. Is neat handwriting important or not? Why? -> Accept students' own asnwers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
filmhabitstudytalk about
B
neatwritingmessyquicklyslowly
C
through

📐 Grammar Focus

1. General questions in the simple present with short answers: move be, or add do/does + base verb. 2. Asking about the underlined part: find the question word, transform, integrate; “what” replaces the verb.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • He often watches films.

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Adverbs-of-Frequency Poster — display the adverbs-of-frequency poster (GE7 U1C2 materials folder) and chant the scale from never to always with a show-of-hands scale; students point to the adverb that matches each classmate’s sentence.
  2. Good Habit / Bad Habit Sort — give each team a stack of sentence strips (“He often copies quickly.”, “She usually studies through the night.”, “He never keeps his room neat.”, “She often talks about films slowly.”); teams sort them into GOOD HABIT and BAD HABIT piles and must debate and justify each one — some are ambiguous; the discussion recycles the target structure.
  3. Feed the Monster (HTML Interactive) -- the students make sentences based on emoji, and then decide if this is a good habit, a bad habit or just an okay habit. The correct sentence make the monster fuller and fuller. (see below)

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: write three sentences about a family member with often, usually or every day, and read them aloud at home.

Classroom Rules

Unit 1 · School Life

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a rules exchange: each student independently asks “Should you [ask for help]?” and answers “Yes, you should.” / “No, you shouldn’t.” with a target rule.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Problem Situation] Noisy Class: the co-teacher mimes a very noisy student (shouting, running, standing on a chair); the teacher tries to start the lesson and keeps getting interrupted (“I want to ask…!”); the co-teacher answers wrong, or just makes noises; the teacher asks "What can we tell to [co-teacher's name]?" Students call out what should happen in any language; land on “You shouldn’t shout in class.”
  • [Interactive Game] Rule or Not?: the teacher mimes short classroom actions (raising a hand, shouting, reading aloud, running, asking a friend for help); students show thumbs up (good) or thumbs down (bad) — accept any language, then give the English sentence (“You should read aloud.” / “You shouldn’t run in class.”).

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. When the teacher is talking, should you make noise? -> No, you shouldn't.
  • 2. Should you read loudly in class? -> Yes, you should.
B
  • 1. What should you do in Bilingo? -> Accept students' own answers. 2. What are classroom rules? -> These are the things you should and shouldn't do when in class.
  • 2. Why Laki can't read loudly? -> Because he's shy.
C
  • 2. Why do we have classroom rules? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
ask forhelploudlyquietlynoisepupilruleunderstand
B
pay attentionread aloud

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Modal should + base verb. 2. Should questions with yes/no answers: “Should you…?” — “Yes/No, …should(n’t).”

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • Should you ask for help? — Yes, you should. / No, you shouldn’t.

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Classroom Rules Sorting (HTML) — project the game (GE7 U1C3 materials folder); situation cards appear one by one (“pay attention to the teacher”, “make noise in class”, “ask for help”); the class decides SHOULD or SHOULDN’T, sorts each card and says the full sentence (“You should ask for help.”). (see below)
  2. Rule Charades — a student draws a rule card (or the teacher whispers one) and mimes it; the class guesses and says the full should/shouldn’t sentence; rotate mimes so every student performs.
  3. Fix the Classroom — the co-teacher mimes small classroom problems (a mess, noise, not understanding); pairs discuss what to do and tell the class “You should…”; the class agrees or offers a different fix.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: write two classroom rules for your room at home with should, and read them to a family member.

Being Polite

Unit 1 · School Life

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a manners exchange: each student independently asks “What should you do when you [know the answer]?” and answers “You should [raise your hand].”.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] From Rules to Manners: quick review of last lesson — the co-teacher mimes shouting in class and the class says “You shouldn’t shout!”; then the teacher mimes knowing the answer (excited face, hand hovering) but staying frozen: “I know! I know! But what do I do?”; the co-teacher answers wrong (“Stand up and shout it!”); students correct in any language; land on “You should raise your hand.”
  • [Topical Discussion] Polite or Not?: mime short scenes (pushing past someone, saying thank you, bumping into someone and saying sorry); students call out good or bad in any language, then connect them to the manners words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

—

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
answermannersmistakeraisesay sorryscaredshoutthanktry
B
important

📐 Grammar Focus

1. when = at the time that…: “What should you do when you know the answer?” 2. Should wh-questions: asking about the underlined part.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • — You should raise your hand.
B
  • — What should you do when you know the answer?

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. What Should You Do? (HTML) — project the game (GE7 U1C4 materials folder); an emoji situation appears (“you have a ❓”, “someone 👊 you”, “you are 😴”) and the class matches it to the right answer (“raise your 🤚”, “tell the 👩‍🏫”) and says the full sentence (“You should raise your hand.”). (see below)
  2. Manners Mimes — a student draws a manners card (or the teacher whispers one) and mimes the polite action; the class guesses the word (say sorry, thank, share) and says the full should sentence.
  3. Polite Fixes — the teacher or co-teacher mimes a rude mini-scene (grab a book, push in line, ignore a greeting); pairs quickly agree on a polite fix and act it out for the class with “You should…”.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: write or say two “What should you do when…?” questions for your family, and answer them together.

The Dance

Unit 5 · The Big Night

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a comparison exchange: each student independently asks “Who is better at [activity], [A] or [B]?” and answers “[A] is better at [activity] than [B].” and “[B] is worse at [activity] than [A].”, using the target activity words.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Problem Situation] Wrong Winner: the teacher and co-teacher mime short talents (e.g. drawing well and drawing badly); the teacher points and asks “Who is better at [drawing], [A] or [B]?” and the co-teacher points at the wrong one on purpose; students call out the right one — accept pointing and any language — then the teacher confirms: “Yes! [A] is better at [dancing] than [B]!”
  • [Topical Discussion] What Are You Good At?: ask students what they are good at (sports, drawing, singing); accept one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the comparison words (for example, invite a student to draw on the whiteboard, and draw very badly next to his picture; ask "Who is better at drawing, [student name] or [teacher name]?"

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. [Point at the host]. What does he do? -> He's a host.
  • 2. Who is better at dancing, Ola or Pili? -> Pili is better at dancing than Ola.
B
  • 1. Is Pili better at dancing now? Why? -> Yes, he is. Because Lele is the best teacher.
  • 2. Did the crowd like the dance? Why? -> Yes, they did. Because they cheer.
C
  • 1. Pili was bad at dancing, but Lele taught him, and now he's better at dancing. What is something you are better at now? -> Accept students' own answers.
  • 2. How did Lele teach Pili to dance? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
cheerjudgewonderful
B
hostintroducelook forward toscorestagewelcome
C
contestant

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Be + better at + noun/verb-ing + than + (another person): “Lele is better at dancing than Pili.” 2. Be + worse at + noun/verb-ing + than + (another person): “Pili is worse at dancing than Lele.”

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • — Who is better at [activity], [A] or [B]?
  • — [A] is better at [activity] than [B].
  • — [B] is worse at [activity] than [A].

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Show-Off (HTML) — Project the game. Two GE characters compete in a random talent (dancing, fishing, doing magic…). The screen shows only two word chips — better (or worse) + the activity — and the class must make the question themselves: “Who is better at fishing, Pili or Laki?” Reveal the question, kids tap the winner, judges pop up with score signs, then the class makes the answer: “Pili is better at fishing than Laki.” Repeat with worse, then tap “Next Show” for a new round. (see below)
  2. Talent Interview — In pairs, students ask each other “What are you better at, [dancing] or [singing]?” and answer with both sentences: “I am better at [dancing] than [singing]. I am worse at [singing] than [dancing].” Then each student reports one answer about a partner to the class.
  3. Better/Worse Ball Toss — ask "Who is better at dancing?" throw the ball to a student who raised their hand. The student should say the target sentence, comparing themselves to a classmate or a teacher, then ask the question for another activity.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: ask a family member “What are you better at, … or …?” and report both sentences to the class.

The Song

Unit 5 · The Big Night

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a superlative exchange: each student independently asks “Who is the loudest?” and answers “[Laki] is the loudest.”, using the -est forms of the target adjectives.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Problem Situation] Three Noises: the teacher challenges the co-teacher to see who can make the noise louder, then ask students if anyone can be even louder. Then, the teacher asks “Who is loud? I am louder than my co-teacher, but student is louder than me... Hmm, so who is the LOUDEST?”; students point and shout their answers in any language — accept everything, then land on the statement: “[Student name] is the loudest?” (Alternatively, challenge the whole class and see who is the loudest in the whole class: "[Student name] is louder than me, and you, and you, and you -- and everyone: [student name] is the loudest.")
  • [Interactive Game] Sound Sort: play or make three sounds at different volumes (a stomp, a clap, a shout); students close their eyes and order them loud -> louder -> the loudest.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. Who is the loudest? -> Laki is the loudest.
  • 2. Can everybody in the theater hear them? -> Yes, they can.
B
  • 1. Why are Nani and Laki relieved? -> Because the crowd claps and cheers.
  • 2. Why are Nani and Laki the bravest contestants? -> Because they can keep on singing without a microphone.
C
  • 1. Are you brave or not brave? Why? -> Accept students' own answers.
  • 2. If you are Laki/Nani, are you scared? What do you do? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
worriedtalentedwithoutbowreally
B
performrelievedsuddenly
C
microphonekeep on

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Superlative spelling rules: usually add -est; words ending in -e add -st; consonant + y → i + est; a stressed consonant-vowel-consonant ending doubles the final letter (big → the biggest); multi-syllable words take the most (the most talented). 2. The comes before the superlative adjective: “Laki is the loudest.”

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • — Who is the loudest?
  • — [Laki] is the loudest.

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Who is the Loudest? (HTML) — Project the game. Three characters perform the same act at three different volumes; the class makes the question “Who is the loudest?”, taps the loudest performer, then makes the answer “… is the loudest.” Repeat with new line-ups and new -est adjectives, including the quietest round. (see below)
  2. Singer Scores — Three students mime performing the same song at different volumes; the class asks “Who is the loudest?”; a student judge (or the teacher) awards 1st, 2nd and 3rd place; the class says the full superlative sentence for each place (“Lele is the loudest.”). Rotate tasks, performers and judges.
  3. Superlative Statues — The teacher calls an adjective (tall, brave, cool, cute, etc); students strike a statue pose showing it; the teacher and a student walk the “gallery” together and pick “the most talented statue” etc., saying the full sentence (“[Student's name] is the bravest!”).

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: record three superlative sentences about family members (e.g. “My dad is the tallest.”).

The Story

Unit 5 · The Big Night

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a superlative description: each student independently says “[Ola] is the most [confident].” about the characters, using the most + adjective form.

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] Loudest vs The Most Artistic: Remind the students about the last class (or do the activity again): who is the loudest. What about artistic? Ask three students (or two teachers and a student) to draw something on the white board (with a time limit) and ask the class: "Who is the most artistic?". Land on the statement: "[Student name] is the most artistic."
  • [Topical Discussion] Confident or Shy?: show a title picture and ask students how they feel if they are Ola while performing on stage — excited, worried, confident; accept gestures and one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the feeling words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. What is Ola going to do? -> He's going to tell a story.
  • 2. Who is the most confident contestant? -> Ola is the most confident contestant.
B
  • 1. How do you feel when you are on a stage? -> Accept students' own answers. 2. Is Ola telling a funny or a sad story? -> Ola is telling a funny story.
  • 2. Why does Ola feel confident? -> Because his friends are confident.
C
  • 2. Do you know any jokes? Can you tell it to the class? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
actsmilestarttellend (n.)jokethink about
B
confidentsilly
C
talkative

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Superlative sentence pattern: A + be + the + superlative + range (of/in…): “Ola is the most confident of the three.” 2. Irregular comparatives and superlatives: good → better → the best; bad → worse → the worst; many/much → more → the most; little → less → the least; far → farther/further → the farthest/the furthest.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • [A] is the most [adjective].
  • e.g. Ola is the most confident.
B
  • — Who is the most [adjective]?*

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. Most Confident? (HTML) — Project the game. Three characters act out a scene (telling jokes, talking non-stop, singing…); the class makes the question “Who is the most [adjective]?” and the answer “… is the most [adjective].”, then taps the character that fits; each round ends with a comparative → superlative spelling ladder for the adjective used. (see below)
  2. Character Awards — Hold a mini awards show for the story characters: “the most confident”, “the silliest”, “the most talkative”; the class says each sentence with the target structure, then gives kind superlative awards to classmates (“the most helpful”).
  3. Story Retell Relay — Groups of four retell the story, one sentence each; every retell must include at least one superlative sentence; the class cheers after each retell; rotate who starts.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: write or say three “the most …” sentences about classmates or family members.

The Winner

Unit 5 · The Big Night

💡 Main Idea — what students should be able to do

Demonstrate a final-round exchange: each student independently asks “Who is the most [adjective] in the competition?” and answers “[Nani] is the most [adjective].”

🎯 Lead-in Ideas

  • [Title Slide Talk] Show the title slide and ask some A-level questions.
  • [Review Bridge] The Finalists: review the superlatives from the unit with quick mimes or flashcards — “Laki is the loudest!” — then announce that tonight is the final of the competition and only three finalists remain: “Who is the best in the competition? How do we ask about all of them at once?”; take hints in any language from the students and land on “Who is the most musical in the competition?”
  • [Topical Discussion] Winners: ask students about competitions they have seen or taken part in — running, singing, drawing contests; accept one-word answers in any language, then connect them to the competition words.

📖 Story Questions (samples)

A
  • 1. Who is the winner in the competition and why? -> Ola is the winner because he is so confident and funny
  • 2. Who is the most musical in the competition? -> Nani is the most musical.
B
  • 1. Do you want to win a competition? What kind of competition? -> Accept students' own answers.
  • 2. Why does the audence love Ola? -> Because he's confident and funny.
C
  • 1. Why does Ola want to share his prize with his friends? -> Because he's proud to have good friends and he feels confident when he watches them
  • 2. Why do kids say "Good luck" and shake hands? -> Accept students' own answers.

🎯 Target Vocabulary

A
cheer (n.)winnerdecideend (v.)
B
congratulationsproudshake handsshout (n.)surprise
C
competition

📐 Grammar Focus

1. Superlative question and answer with a range: “Who is the + superlative + range (in the competition / in our class)?” — “A is the + superlative.” 2. Irregular superlatives (review): good → the best; bad → the worst; many/much → the most.

🗣 Target Structures

A
  • — Who is the most [adjective] in the competition?
  • — [Nani] is the most [adjective].

🎲 Bonus Activity Ideas

  1. And the Winner Is… (HTML) — Project the game. Three finalists perform on stage (singing, juggling, dancing…); the class asks “Who is the most [adjective] in the competition?” and taps the finalist whose act fits the category; the host announces the winner; the class cheers “Congratulations!”, shakes hands and says “… is the most [adjective].” (see below)
  2. Award Ceremony (printout) — Use the role cards and score paddles (GE7 U5C17. Judge Paddles & Role Cards.pdf): the host announces “The winner is…!”, the winner says “Thank you!”, and the class cheers “Congratulations!”; the ceremony repeats with new categories and rotated roles.
  3. Superlative Survey — In row groups, students ask “Who is the most …?” about their own group (tallest, funniest, most musical); each group reports its findings to the class with full superlative sentences.

🏠 Homework Ideas

  1. Using the teacher's example, record a dialogue with your parents or friends using a target structure.
  2. Read the words, the dialogue and the story from the book. Extension 1: make your own sentences with the target vocabulary and record them for the teacher. Extension 2: read the story with expression and/or role play the story with your friends or parents.
  3. Extension: hold a mini award ceremony at home — name a winner and say why: “… is the most …!”
